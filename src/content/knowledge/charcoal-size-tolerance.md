## Quick Answer

Size tolerance is the permitted deviation of individual charcoal pieces from a nominal size — for example a "25 mm" cube that is allowed to vary by ±0.5 mm. It matters because consistency drives two commercial outcomes at once: how pieces pack into a fixed carton, and how evenly a batch burns. A nominal size without a stated tolerance is an unfinished specification.

**Related data & testing:** [Size tolerance specification](https://data.guotan.com/specifications/size-tolerance/) · [Size tolerance test method](https://testing.guotan.com/tests/size-tolerance/)

## Key findings

- **A nominal size is a target, not a guarantee.** Two suppliers quoting "25 mm" can ship noticeably different products if the tolerance differs.
- **Tolerance drives carton fill.** If pieces are sold by count per carton, tolerance determines whether count and weight can both be true.
- **Tolerance drives burn evenness.** Undersized pieces burn out while oversized pieces are still going, so a wide spread heats a bowl or grill unevenly.
- **Tighter tolerance costs more.** It means more rejected output, so it is a commercial trade-off, not a free improvement.
- **Measurement must be specified** — which dimension, calipers versus gauge, and the sampling plan — or the figure is not comparable.

## What the metric means

A nominal size class such as 25 mm or 26 mm is a target dimension. Real production varies, and tolerance is the band of variation the buyer accepts and the producer can hold. A common band is ±0.5 mm, but the actual band is whatever is written into the purchase specification — and it should be written, not assumed.

The relationship to piece count is direct: a cube's mass scales with volume, so a size class also implies a pieces-per-kilogram count. Public size-class tables give roughly 72 cubes per kg for 25 mm and roughly 64 for 26 mm — numbers that only hold if the pieces actually conform to the class.

## Why it matters commercially

| Area | Effect of loose tolerance |
| --- | --- |
| Carton fill | Count and weight stop agreeing; cartons over- or under-fill |
| Burn evenness | Mixed sizes burn at different rates; uneven heat |
| Container loading | Inconsistent piece size changes packing density |
| Customer returns | Visible size variation drives complaints |

For a hookah lounge or retail brand, uneven size is a quality complaint that no chemical spec sheet will catch — it is visible and tactile, and it changes the session.

## How size is checked

There is no single universal charcoal-specific standard for cube dimensions, so producers and buyers agree the nominal size, the sampling plan and the deviation in the purchase specification. The practical check is dimensional measurement:

- A random sample of pieces measured with **calibrated calipers** or a go/no-go gauge.
- The relevant dimension measured — edge length for cubes.
- A summary reported as **mean and range**, not a single "25 mm" claim.

For fragmented or granular material, sieve analysis against a declared size class applies instead (ISO 17225 series and ASTM D4749 are the public method families). For cubes, calipers on a real sample is the honest check.

## How to specify it in writing

1. State the **nominal size** and the **permitted deviation** (e.g. 25 mm ± 0.5 mm).
2. State the **sampling plan** — how many pieces, drawn how.
3. Ask for a **mean-and-range summary** from a random sample, not a single claimed size.
4. Add an **arrival check** if pieces are packed in a way that can abrade or break during transit.

## Conditional recommendations

- **If you run a lounge**, require a published tolerance and sample-test with calipers; evenness is your service quality.
- **If you distribute retail**, tolerance is a returns-driver — a tight, enforced tolerance protects your margin.
- **If you are price-driven**, accept a wider tolerance but specify a floor so you still know what you are buying.
- **If size is genuinely critical**, be prepared to pay for tighter tolerance; it is not free at the factory.

## Limitations and uncertainty

Tolerance measured at the factory is a snapshot of one sample; breakage and moisture during transport can shift the distribution by arrival. Measurement results depend on how pieces are measured and how the sample is drawn. This article states no supplier tolerance and no measured result.
