## Quick Answer

There is no single "charcoal certificate." A charcoal import runs on a set of documents that each prove something different: a certificate of origin covers duty and origin, a third-party test report verifies measured batch properties, and dangerous-goods documents (UN 1361, Class 4.2) are mandatory transport paperwork. What a destination requires is specific — EUDR, REACH and Section 301 duties each do different things, and requirements may apply depending on the destination and change frequently. This describes document categories, not legal advice; confirm the exact document set with your customs broker.

**Related data & testing:** [Specification database](https://data.chinacharcoalhub.com/) · [Test methodologies](https://testing.chinacharcoalhub.com/)

## Key findings

- **A certificate of origin is about duty and origin, not quality.** It proves where the goods came from, not how they perform.
- **Third-party testing is the document that separates verified claims from supplier claims** — but only if the laboratory is independent and the report covers the batch you bought.
- **Fumigation and phytosanitary documents** address packing-material and pest risk, usually driven by wooden pallets.
- **Dangerous-goods documentation is mandatory** because charcoal ships as UN 1361, Class 4.2 — this is transport law, not an optional extra.
- **Destination requirements are specific.** EUDR, REACH questions and Section 301 duties each do different things; none is a single universal certificate.

## The document categories

| Document | What it proves | What it does not prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Certificate of Origin (CO) | Country of origin for duty/tariff | Product quality or safety |
| Commercial Invoice (CI) | Value and terms of sale | Origin or compliance |
| Packing List (PL) | Quantities, weights, packaging | Quality or origin |
| Bill of Lading (BL) | Carriage and title | Product quality |
| Fumigation / phytosanitary | Pest treatment of packing material | Product compliance |
| Dangerous Goods Declaration + MSDS | Transport-safety classification | Market product compliance |
| Third-party test report | Measured batch properties | Compliance with a market's product rules |

The key distinction: transport documents get the goods *to* the border, and market-compliance documents get them *through* it. They are not interchangeable.

## Certificate of origin

A CO states the country of origin, which determines duty treatment under the destination's tariff schedule — HTS heading 4402 in the US, TARIC heading 4402 in the EU. It is an origin document, not a quality statement. A CO that is clean tells you nothing about ash or moisture.

## Third-party testing

This is the document that earns trust. A third-party laboratory report on the batch you are buying — ash, moisture, fixed carbon, volatile matter — is the difference between a supplier's claim and a verified figure. Two conditions matter:

1. **The laboratory must be independent of the supplier.**
2. **The report must cover your batch**, not a different one.

Inspection and testing bodies (for example SGS and similar independent testing houses) are widely used in commodity trade for pre-shipment inspection and analysis, but the value of the report depends on its independence, scope and batch traceability. A supplier-generated PDF is not the same thing. For the methodology behind the figures, see [how to compare coconut shell charcoal](/knowledge/how-to-compare-coconut-shell-charcoal/).

## Fumigation and phytosanitary documents

Where packing material — most commonly wooden pallets — carries pest risk, the destination may require fumigation or phytosanitary treatment and a certificate for it. This is a packing-material requirement, not a charcoal-quality requirement, but it is frequently the document that delays clearance when it is missing.

## Dangerous-goods documentation

Charcoal ships as **UN 1361, "Carbon, animal or vegetable origin", Class 4.2 (self-heating)**, packing groups II or III. This triggers a dangerous-goods declaration and a material safety data sheet as a matter of transport law, independent of any market's import rules. See [charcoal container loading](/knowledge/charcoal-container-loading/) and [how to source charcoal from China](/knowledge/how-to-source-charcoal-from-china/) for the practical implications.

## Destination-specific requirements

- **European Union** — EUDR (Regulation (EU) 2023/1115) applies to listed commodities including wood charcoal under heading 4402; coconut shell charcoal's treatment depends on the declared subheading and import context, and application dates have shifted (to 30 December 2026 for large/medium operators and downstream traders, 30 June 2027 for micro/small enterprises, per Regulation (EU) 2025/2650). REACH may also apply. This is not a "certificate" in the CO sense — it is a due-diligence and declaration obligation.
- **United Kingdom** — the UK runs its own chemicals regime (UK REACH) and its own deforestation-due-diligence approach post-Brexit.
- **United States** — HTS classification (heading 4402) plus the possibility of Section 301 additional duties on Chinese-origin goods; rates change, so verify the current status.

None of these is a single document a supplier simply hands over; each requires the buyer to understand the obligation and hold the right evidence.

## A documentation checklist

1. Confirm the **document set** your destination actually requires — with your broker, not from a supplier's website.
2. Get the **CO, CI, PL, BL** for the shipment.
3. Confirm **dangerous-goods documentation** is complete before booking.
4. Request a **third-party test report for your batch**, not a sample from another run.
5. Confirm **fumigation/phytosanitary** treatment if wooden pallets are used.
6. Build the **EUDR / REACH / Section 301 file** where your market requires it.

## Conditional recommendations

- **If you are a first-time importer**, engage a customs broker early and have them specify the exact document set for your market and HS code.
- **If quality is your differentiator**, insist on an independent third-party report covering your batch.
- **If you ship on wooden pallets**, resolve fumigation/phytosanitary documents before the goods move.
- **If you sell into the EU**, treat EUDR as a due-diligence obligation to document, not a single certificate to buy.

## Limitations and uncertainty

Documentary requirements and their legal effect vary by market and change frequently. This article describes general document categories from public sources, states no certification for any product, and is not legal advice. Re-verify the current official text and consult your broker before contracting.
