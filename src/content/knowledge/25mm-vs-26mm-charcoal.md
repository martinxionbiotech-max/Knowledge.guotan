## Quick Answer

25 mm and 26 mm cubes are close enough to look interchangeable and different enough to change a session. The 26 mm cube carries roughly 12–13% more material, so it tends to burn longer and hold heat more stably, while the 25 mm cube fits tighter bowls and heat-management devices more easily. Size is nominal: a "25 mm" product is a target dimension inside a tolerance, and if the tolerance is unpublished the specification is incomplete. The right choice is dictated by hardware and session length, not by a universal "better".

## Key findings

- **Size is nominal.** A "25 mm" product is a target dimension inside a tolerance; if the tolerance is unpublished, the spec is incomplete.
- **Mass scales with volume.** A cube 4% larger per edge carries roughly 12–13% more material, which is why burn and heat shift.
- **Fit beats theory.** If a cube does not seat in your device or bowl, the burn argument is irrelevant.
- **Tolerance is a cost driver.** Tighter tolerances mean more rejected output and usually a higher price; looser tolerances raise the risk of inconsistent sessions.
- **Packaging changes the math.** Different cube counts per box change both shelf presentation and container loading.

## The dimensional question

A cube is defined by three edges, and real production varies in all three. Two things matter commercially:

1. **Nominal size** — the target, e.g. 25 mm.
2. **Tolerance** — the permitted deviation, e.g. ±0.5 mm or ±1 mm.

Without a stated tolerance, two suppliers quoting "25 mm" may be shipping noticeably different products. The only way to know is to measure a sample with calipers across many cubes, not one.

## Heat and burn trade-offs

Because mass scales with volume, a small edge difference compounds.

| Attribute | 25 mm cube | 26 mm cube | Practical effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Volume (approx.) | 15.6 cm³ | 17.6 cm³ | ~12–13% more material |
| Thermal mass | Lower | Higher | More stable heat, slower response |
| Typical burn | Shorter | Longer | Session length |
| Bowl/device fit | Easier | Tighter | Hardware compatibility |
| Heat control | Faster to adjust | Slower to adjust | Management style |

These are geometric consequences, not measured burn results. Treat the direction as reliable and the magnitude as product-specific.

## Bowl fit and heat management

Heat-management devices, chimney bowls and traditional foil setups all impose a geometry. A cube that is too large can overhang, block airflow, or fail to seat flush; a cube that is too small leaves gaps that change airflow. Before choosing a size, test both in *your* hardware with *your* typical number of coals. This is a one-evening test that prevents a container of regret.

## Packaging and loading implications

Cube size changes packaging in two ways:

- **Count per box.** A fixed-weight box holds fewer larger cubes; retail presentation and price-per-piece shift.
- **Volumetrics.** Packing density and box dimensions feed directly into how many tonnes fit a container — see [charcoal container loading](/knowledge/charcoal-container-loading/).

Neither is decisive on its own, but together they can move delivered cost more than the size itself.

## Conditional recommendations

- **If you run a hookah lounge** with standard bowls and high throughput, 25 mm is the safer default because it fits more hardware and gives faster heat adjustment.
- **If you serve long sessions or heat-managed setups**, 26 mm gives more thermal buffer and fewer mid-session coal changes.
- **If you are a distributor** serving mixed hardware, stock the size your top customer base uses and require a published tolerance so returns stay low.
- **If your device is unusual**, buy samples in both sizes and test before committing to a printed specification.

## Limitations and uncertainty

The trade-offs above are derived from geometry and public industry guidance; they are not measured test results. Real behaviour depends on density, ash and hardware. Because tolerances are often unpublished, always request the tolerance in writing and verify a sample.
