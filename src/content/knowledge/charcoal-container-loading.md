## Quick Answer

Charcoal is loaded into standard containers either floor-stuffed (bags stacked directly inside) or palletised, with tonnage set by a tug-of-war between weight limits and volume. A 20-foot container of retail-packaged coconut shell charcoal typically carries roughly 17–20 tonnes floor-stuffed and less when palletised; a 40-foot high-cube loads roughly 25–28 tonnes. The real constraint is often not weight but how densely the product packs — and how dangerous-goods rules (UN 1361, Class 4.2) restrict the load.

**Related data & testing:** [Specification database](https://data.guotan.com/) · [Test methodologies](https://testing.guotan.com/)

## Key findings

- **Charcoal is a volume-constrained cargo.** Its low bulk density means the container often "cubes out" before it "weighs out".
- **Packaging decides tonnage.** Bulk bags pack denser than retail boxes, which waste volume and add breakage risk.
- **Palletising costs capacity.** Pallets consume 15–20% of usable volume in public estimates, directly cutting tonnes per container.
- **Dangerous-goods rules shape the load.** Charcoal is UN 1361, Class 4.2 (self-heating), and recent IMDG editions require it declared as dangerous goods.
- **Loading method matters.** Hand-stuffing packs denser than machine loading but is slower and costlier.

## The two constraints: weight and volume

A dry 20-foot general-purpose container has a payload limit around the high-20-tonne range and an internal volume around 33 m³. A 40-foot high-cube roughly doubles the volume. Charcoal's low bulk density means volume usually binds first: you fill the container before you reach the weight limit. That is why packing density is the single biggest lever on cost per tonne delivered.

Source: public industry loading guides (see Sources). Ranges are reported, not guaranteed, and vary with packing density and loading method.

Some exporters also describe a 20-foot load of 17–20 tonnes for shisha charcoal depending on size and packaging, and around 25.5 tonnes for a 40-foot high-cube — consistent with the ranges above but not identical, which is exactly why you should not bank on a single number.

## Floor-stuffing vs palletising

| Method | Effect on capacity | Trade-offs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Floor-stuffed (hand) | Highest tonnage | Labour-intensive, slower loading, goods not unitised |
| Floor-stuffed (machine) | High tonnage | Needs equipment at the loading point |
| Palletised | 15–20% capacity loss | Faster discharge, cleaner handling, protects goods |

Palletising is a service decision as much as a capacity one: you trade tonnes per container for speed and lower damage at destination. For high-volume commodity flows, floor-stuffing usually wins on cost; for fragile retail packaging, palletising can pay for itself in reduced claims.

## Packaging choices and their consequences

- **Bulk / loose bags:** densest packing, lowest packaging cost, but no retail presentation and higher moisture exposure.
- **Retail inner boxes:** better presentation and protection, but boxes add air and reduce tonnes per container.
- **Printed bags / private label:** strongest brand presence, but adds a print minimum and lengthens lead time (see [coconut charcoal MOQ](/knowledge/coconut-charcoal-moq/)).

Moisture control matters throughout: charcoal can take up moisture in humid conditions, which adds dead weight and hurts ignition. Specify packaging that limits moisture uptake and store accordingly.

## Dangerous-goods constraints

Charcoal ships as **UN 1361, "Carbon, animal or vegetable origin", Class 4.2 (substances liable to spontaneous combustion)**, packing groups II or III. Under recent IMDG Code amendments, charcoal must be declared and carried as dangerous goods; the older self-heating-test exemption path has been withdrawn. Practically, this means:

- a dangerous-goods declaration and a current material safety data sheet,
- packing and stowage that follow the code,
- evidence of adequate weathering/maturation before loading,
- a carrier that accepts Class 4.2 cargo.

This is a documentation and stowage discipline, not just a label. A mis-declared container can be refused or held, and the underlying risk — self-heating in a sealed box — is why the rules exist.

## A practical load checklist

1. Confirm the **packaging specification** and expected bags/pallets per container.
2. Get the **load plan in tonnes and in units**, from the supplier.
3. Verify **dangerous-goods documentation** is in order before booking.
4. Check **moisture and weathering evidence** for the batch.
5. Confirm **weight and volume limits** with the forwarder for the specific container type.
6. Photograph the **stuffing** and record seal numbers.

## Conditional recommendations

- **If freight cost per tonne is your priority**, choose bulk packaging and floor-stuffing, and accept slower discharge.
- **If retail presentation matters**, expect fewer tonnes per container and plan packaging around that capacity loss.
- **If your destination disputes damages**, palletising can reduce claims even at lower tonnage.
- **If the route involves long voyage times**, prioritise weathering evidence and moisture-controlled packaging over marginal density gains.

## Limitations and uncertainty

Reported ranges come from public industry guides and differ between sources because products, packaging and methods differ. Actual tonnage depends on the specific product and load plan. Always confirm the load with the supplier and forwarder, and re-check current IMDG requirements with a dangerous-goods specialist before shipping.
